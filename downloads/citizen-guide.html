Citizen's guide

Understanding India's economy in simple terms
What the data shows, and why it matters to you

Dear fellow citizen,

You have probably heard that India is the "fastest-growing economy" and wondered why your family still struggles with rising prices, job searches, and stagnant incomes. This guide explains what the data shows, in simple language, with examples from daily life.

You do not need an economics degree to follow it. It takes about 15 minutes to read, and it explains how government policies affect your income, your job, and your future.

Part 1: What GDP is, and why it confuses people

GDP = Total Value of Everything Produced
Think of it as adding up everything made in India, from cars to chapatis.

An example

An imagined example; the figures are made up.
A factory made 100 cars in 2014 and 110 in 2024, so its output grew 10%. That is the kind of growth the GDP figures report, because they take out price rises.
• The factory employed 1,000 workers in 2014.
• It now employs 500, because of automation.
So output went up and jobs went down.

Common belief: high GDP growth means everyone is better off.
What the data allows: GDP can grow while most people get poorer, if the benefits go to the top.

Part 2: The jobs crisis

1 in 10
Indian workers is a regular employee with any social-security cover, such as a provident fund or pension (10.1% in 2023-24, Periodic Labour Force Survey). The survey records no cover for the self-employed and casual workers who make up most of the rest.

Your father's generation (1990s)

  • A government job meant lifetime security.
  • A private job came with a provident fund and a pension.

Your generation (2020s)

  • NITI Aayog's 2022 report on the gig economy found that most platform workers had no social-security cover. The Code on Social Security, in force since 21 November 2025, provides for schemes for them, funded by levies on the platforms.
  • A contract job comes with no benefits.
  • About 1 in 10 workers has a regular job with social security (10.1% in 2023-24, down from 11.5% in 2017-18).

Example: a delivery worker

An imagined example; Raju is not a real person and the figures are illustrative.
Raju delivers food for an app. He works 12 hours a day and earns ₹15,000 a month.
• He gets no paid leave when he is sick.
• He has no provident fund for retirement.
• He has no job security, and can be "logged out" at any time.
• He pays for his own petrol and bike maintenance.
He is counted as "employed", and he has no real job security.

Part 3: Why the rich get richer

₹100
In 2022-23, for every ₹100 of income earned in India, about ₹23 went to the richest 1% of adults and ₹15 to the poorer half (World Inequality Lab)

Top 1% of adults ₹18 ₹23
Bottom 50% of adults ₹19 ₹15

An imagined example: housing

The figures here are made up to show the mechanism. They are not measured prices.
Imagine a flat that costs five years of a middle-class salary.
If buyers with spare wealth buy several such flats as investments, prices rise faster than salaries.
After some years the same flat costs ten years of salary, and a family that earns only a salary finds it harder to buy one.

Part 4: How much of your tax reaches your state

₹34 out of ₹100
is what the states received from every ₹100 of tax the Union collected in 2024-25. The Finance Commission gives states ₹41 of every ₹100 in the shared pool, but cesses and surcharges are kept out of that pool, and in 2024-25 they were ₹18 of every ₹100.

How the Union shares its taxes

Of every ₹100 the Union collected in taxes in 2024-25, about ₹18 came from cesses and surcharges. Cesses and surcharges sit outside the pool the Union shares with states, so no state receives any of them. States together received about ₹34 of the ₹100 as their share of central taxes. Fuel duties are a large part of the cess money, because most of the Union's excise on petrol is levied as special additional duty and cess. (Union Receipt Budget and Budget at a Glance 2026-27, actuals for 2024-25.)

Common belief: states are fiscally irresponsible.
What the data shows: states get less money and carry more responsibilities (schools, hospitals, police).

Part 5: Why good data is hard to find

No census since 2011
India is planning for 2026 with 2011 population data. The next count is due in February 2027.

The work that never shows up in GDP

Here is something odd about how the economy gets measured. If you own your flat, the government works out roughly what rent you would have paid a landlord, and adds that to GDP. You paid nobody, and it still counts.

Now think about the person who cooked dinner in that flat, minded the children, and sat up with a sick parent. None of that goes into GDP. It is recorded as zero.

The government knows how much of this work happens, because it counts the hours. In 2024 its Time Use Survey found that women aged 6 and above who do this work spend 4 hours 49 minutes a day on it. Men who do it spend 1 hour 28 minutes. For a woman that is around 1,758 hours a year, roughly a full-time job, and it goes in no employment figure and no growth figure.

Estimates of what it would be worth if somebody paid for it range from 7.5% of GDP to 36%, depending on who you ask and how they price an hour. Nobody has settled the question, because no government agency has been told to.

This matters for a number you may have heard. About 40% of Indian women aged 15 and over are counted in the labour force (PLFS, 2025), and 38.8% are counted as working. The rest get talked about as women not working. The 2024 Time Use Survey found 81.5% of women aged 6 and above doing unpaid domestic work at home on the day they were asked about. These women are working, and the accounts do not add up the kind of work they do.

Example: your colony

An imagined example; the figures are made up.
Suppose your colony had 1,000 families in 2011 and has 3,000 now.
Any plan that still rests on the 2011 count would provide water, power and school places for a third of the people who live there.

Q: Why was important data hidden?

A leaked draft of the 2017-18 consumption survey, reported by Business Standard in November 2019, showed real spending per person lower than in 2011-12. The government scrapped the survey that month, citing the quality of the data, and no figures from it were published. Hiding data that shows a problem does not solve the problem.

Part 6: What you can do

1. Vote with the facts

  • Ask candidates about local employment as well as GDP.
  • Ask for data on the jobs created as well as the roads built.
  • Ask why your state gets less tax money.

2. Ask for transparency

  • File RTI requests about local development funds.
  • Ask for employment data for your district.
  • Ask why the census and surveys are missing.

3. Support the local economy

  • Buy from local shops as well as large online sellers.
  • Support small businesses that create jobs.
  • Choose products made by local MSMEs.

4. Talk about it

  • Share this guide with friends and family.
  • Discuss the economy beyond the stock market.
  • Check official claims against the data.

Common questions

Q: But aren't we becoming a $5 trillion economy?

Yes. If onions cost ₹200 a kilo in that economy, you may still be worse off. For a household, how income is distributed matters more than the total, and a bigger economy does a family little good if its share keeps shrinking.

Q: Why should I care about inequality?

High inequality makes education costlier, because the rich bid up prices. It makes healthcare more expensive, because they can pay more. It costs jobs, because wealth goes into capital that employs few workers. And it weakens democracy, because money buys influence.

Q: Isn't some data suppression normal?

No. Democracies depend on information. When a government hides data, it hides problems, and a problem that is not measured cannot be fixed.

Three simple lessons

Lesson 1: Inflation eats growth

If your salary rises 5% and prices rise 7%, you are about 2% poorer. This is why "growth" does not always mean prosperity.

Lesson 2: Jobs and employment

A job means a regular salary, benefits and security.
Employment means any work, including selling vegetables.
India needs jobs, and employment alone does not supply them.

Lesson 3: Fiscal federalism

Your state provides schools, hospitals and police.
The Centre controls most of the tax money.
It is like your parents keeping your salary and asking you to pay the rent.

Key takeaways

Five facts to remember

  1. GDP growth does not mean your prosperity has grown.
  2. About one worker in ten is a regular employee with any social-security cover (10.1% in 2023-24).
  3. The richest 1% of adults receive more income than the poorer half put together (22.6% against 15.0% in 2022-23).
  4. States receive about ₹34 of every ₹100 the Union collects in tax.
  5. Data suppression hides real problems.

Why this matters

Democracy works when citizens are informed. Use what you have read here to ask questions of the people who govern you, and to expect answers.
