What changed, and when

Updates

A plain-language record of what changed on this site and when. Data on this site is revised as official releases land, and corrections are made in public. This page exists to make both visible. Each entry corresponds to a published version of the site. The full technical detail, including internal refactors not worth listing here, is in the repository's CHANGELOG.

2026-10-12  ·  v2.55.0

Forecast arithmetic and the site's own projections

What the published forecasts do to the comparison, and back-tested projections for series nobody else forecasts.

  • Added: The forecasts page now shows what each published GDP forecast for 2026-27 does to the NDA-period average. With any of them the average stays below the UPA decade's 6.82%; matching it would need 14.3% growth in 2026-27.
  • Added: The site's own projections, to 2026, for three series no institution forecasts: the top 1% and bottom 50% income shares and formal-job coverage. Three simple models are scored by a rolling-origin back-test, the winner is used, and each projection carries an 80% interval built from its past errors. They are drawn as hollow markers with a shaded interval and never enter an average. The site does not forecast GDP or inflation itself.

2026-10-10  ·  v2.51.0

The three indices and the fiscal and jobs claims checked against their publishers

Every input to the three indices was checked against its publisher. The fiscal index was rebuilt from Finance Commission, Budget and RBI figures and now tells a narrower story: the NDA period still scores as more centralised, but because of GST and the 2020 borrowing conditions, while devolution to states rose after 2015. The NDA period remains worse than the UPA decade on eight of nine comparable measures.

  • Fixed: The Fiscal Centralisation Index used inputs that matched no published series. Devolution was shown near 35% of gross tax revenue under UPA and falling under NDA. The 16th Finance Commission's figures are 25.8% in 2004-05 and 27.9% in 2013-14, rising to 34.8% in 2015-16 after the 14th Finance Commission and 33.9% in 2024-25. States' own revenue was shown 15 to 20 points too low, and conditional grants were shown rising steadily to 42% of transfers, where the published share moves between 27% and 40%. The index now uses the published figures and leaves empty the years with none. It averages 0.30 under UPA and 0.43 under NDA, peaks at 0.87 in 2020 and is 0.35 in 2026. On the four measured fiscal ratios alone the eras are close (0.49 and 0.45), so the higher NDA score comes from GST and the 2020 borrowing conditions.
  • Fixed: The cess and surcharge share of gross tax revenue was described as rising from 8% to 29%. The 15th Finance Commission and the Union Receipt Budgets put it at 10.4% in 2011-12, a peak of 24.4% in 2020-21 and 18.0% in 2024-25. The cess chart and its data file now use these figures.
  • Fixed: Freedom House scores in the Democratic Quality Index were too high for the UPA years (78-79 against published 76-77) and for 2024-2025 (66 against 63 and 62). RSF's 2012 rank was the 2013 rank repeated; RSF's 2011-12 index gives 131. V-Dem's two series match its March 2026 release exactly. The DQI for 2026 is 0.28 and the NDA average 0.37.
  • Changed: The census is scored as a scheduled delay in 2026 (houselisting from April 2026, enumeration notified for February 2027), so the Statistical Suppression Index for 2026 is 4.65 in place of 6.4.
  • Fixed: Employment elasticity of 0.01 was attributed to 2014-17 and called the lowest since independence. RBI puts 0.01 at 2004-05 to 2009-10, and ORF puts it at 2011-16 with 1.11 for 2017-23. The technical appendix's elasticity table, whose confidence intervals and R-squared values nothing had computed, is replaced by the published estimates. Two unsourced figures, '80% of new jobs are precarious' and '45% of youth underemployed', are replaced by the ILO and IHD India Employment Report 2024.
  • Fixed: The methodology document described analyses the project never ran (difference-in-differences, synthetic control, structural-break tests, bootstrap intervals, external-validation correlations) and a replication guide for files that do not exist. It now describes what the project does.
  • Fixed: The downloadable master dataset was a separate hand-kept copy that disagreed with the site and carried columns with no source. It is now written from the site's data by a script and checked in CI, and the replication scripts in Python and R run against it and reproduce the eight-of-nine comparison.
  • Fixed: The rupee series was checked against the Federal Reserve's H.10 monthly averages; 2023 is 83 in place of 82.

Two more findings rested on figures that did not match their sources. Both still show the NDA years doing worse than the UPA years, by less than the site said.

  • Fixed: Formal employment was shown falling from 13.0% in 2014 to 10.9% in 2026, in ten of twelve years. The site now derives it from PLFS: regular wage and salaried workers as a share of all workers, times the share of those employees with any social security. That gives 11.5% in 2017-18, 9.6% in 2022-23 and 10.1% in 2023-24. PLFS begins in 2017-18, so the other years are empty. The share fell before the pandemic and has recovered part of the loss. 2026-08-15  ·  v2.47.0

The explainers get a front door, and a walkthrough slide

GDP prices the rent on a flat you own and puts nothing against the meal cooked in it. That point now runs through the paper, the methodology, the appendix, the summary, the citizen guide, the policy brief and the analyses. Before this release it sat on one page.

  • Added: A page at someperspective.info/care on the production boundary: what the national accounts count as production, and what they leave outside. The accounts work out a rent for the flat you own and add it to output. They put nothing against the meal cooked in it. The site had one sentence on unpaid work and nothing behind it.
  • Data: The National Statistical Office's two Time Use Surveys, from 2019 and 2024, are now in the dataset. Among people aged 15-59 who do it at all, women spent 289 minutes a day on unpaid domestic work in 2024 against 88 minutes for men. For women that comes to about 1,758 hours a year that appear in no employment statistic and no output figure.
  • Added: Two survey rounds allow the change to be measured, and the result does not point cleanly in either direction. Men's participation nearly doubled, from 26.1% to 45.8%. Minutes fell for both sexes, however, so the gap among participants went from 202 minutes to 201, a change of one minute in five years. Over the same period women's participation in paid employment rose from 21.8% to 25.0%. More women are in paid work, and the unpaid work has not been meaningfully redistributed.
  • Added: Published Indian valuations of unpaid household work run from 7.5% of GDP to 36%, a factor of nearly five. Almost all of that spread comes from the choice of valuation method, and very little from measurement error. The page publishes the full range with each method named and declines to pick a number. Its claim is narrower. Every one of those estimates is large, and none can be called correct because no statistical agency has been asked to settle the question.
  • Changed: This bears on a figure used elsewhere on the site. Female labour force participation of about 21% is usually discussed as an absence of work, while the Time Use Survey records 83.9% of women aged 15-59 doing unpaid domestic work on the reference day. Both are true. Together they show the participation figure measures an absence of work inside the boundary. That is now stated where the employment numbers are used.
  • Note: No figure on this site has been adjusted. Every growth series remains the official one on the official boundary. The claim is that the boundary is a choice, that India already collects the hours needed to value what sits outside it, and that a framework which imputes rent for a dwelling but records zero for the care performed inside it embeds a judgement about whose work counts as production. The research paper carries the same argument as Section 6.11.
  • Changed: The argument now sits in every document. Methodology gains a section under Limitations. The technical appendix gains A4b, with both Time Use Survey rounds tabulated by sex and the five published valuations listed with their methods. The executive summary states before its findings that the growth figures exclude this work. The citizen guide explains it in plain language. The policy brief asks for the specific thing that would fix it, which is a household production satellite account built on the survey hours India already collects. The media kit carries it as a quotable fact. The paper has it as Section 6.11.
  • Added: A new standing analysis, 'GDP counts the rent on your flat and nothing for the cooking', with its own permalink. The library is now 37 pieces, and the count on the site is read from the data instead of being typed in, so it no longer goes stale.
  • Changed: The walkthrough's limits slide now names this too. Every growth figure in the deck sits on the official boundary and none is adjusted.
  • Fixed: Both long-form explainers were badly buried. The GDP page was reachable from one sentence inside another section and a single footer link, and neither page was in the navigation at all. There is now an Explainers menu in the top navigation and a card near the top of the opening section carrying both. The list is held as data, so the next explainer appears in both places from one line.
  • Added: The guided walkthrough gains a slide on it, placed straight after the inflation slide so a reader reaches the verdict holding both caveats about what the headline figure does and does not contain. The deck is now eighteen slides. The bullet that had been added to the limits slide is removed, since the point now has a slide of its own.
  • Fixed: The slide count and the analysis count were typed into the page in several places and had already gone stale twice. Both now come from the data.

2026-08-15  ·  v2.43.0

A dedicated page on why inflation looks better after 2014

The one measure that improved is now argued out at length, on its own page and in a new section of the paper. Until this release it was handled in a paragraph.

  • Added: A standalone explainer at someperspective.info/inflation. Consumer price inflation is the single cross-era measure on which India performs better after 2014, at 4.98% against 8.05%. A project that reports eight findings in its own direction and disposes of the ninth in a sentence has not earned the eight. The page sets out six factors in order of how much each explains, then the strongest case that can be made against its own conclusion, then what evidence would change it.
  • Added: One of those six is new to this project, and it counts against the government. Central excise duty on petrol was ₹9.48 a litre and on diesel ₹3.56 in 2014. Between November 2014 and January 2016 it was raised on nine separate occasions, by ₹11.77 a litre on petrol and ₹13.47 on diesel. The stated purpose was to move the gains from collapsing world oil prices to the exchequer and away from the pump. The disinflation households experienced was therefore the residual after the tax take, and to the extent fiscal policy touched fuel prices in that window it acted to keep them higher than world prices would have delivered.
  • Added: The research paper gains Section 6.10, which makes the same argument at full length with two sub-sections: the strongest case against the paper's own reading, and what would falsify it. A consistent measure of core inflation excluding food and fuel would settle much of it. This project does not have one going back to 2004, and the paper says so plainly.
  • Changed: The one-line thesis, which appears in four places on the site and ends 'Inflation is the one exception', now links straight to the explanation.
